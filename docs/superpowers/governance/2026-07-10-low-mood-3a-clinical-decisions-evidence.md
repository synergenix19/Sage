## CD1 — Sign List A (fire), but tier it by DSM-5 signal strength
**Recommendation: sign the anhedonia/energy markers as strong standalone triggers; mark the low-specificity ones as "weaker — corroboration helpful" rather than standalone.**
- **Strong, specific (route to the screen):** "nothing sounds enjoyable" (anhedonia, a DSM-5 gateway A-criterion), "everything feels like an effort" / "even small tasks feel difficult" (fatigue/energy loss, A6), "going through the motions" (anhedonia-adjacent).
- **Weaker, non-specific (real but overlaps introversion/burnout/stress):** "I don't want to talk to anyone" (social withdrawal is not a standalone DSM criterion), "I keep putting everything off" (procrastination/avolition, non-specific).
Evidence: depressed mood and **anhedonia** are the two DSM-5 gateway symptoms (≥1 required); a single phrase is a *symptom*, not the ≥5-symptom/2-week/impairment disorder — so these justify *further screening*, not a conclusion. Several are explicitly flagged non-specific (shared with grief, stress, normal low mood). ## CD2 — Sign List B (look-alike): the precision boundary is clinically load-bearing
**Recommendation: sign the look-alike set as the precision oracle; it is what prevents over-screening.**
Evidence: high-sensitivity/low-specificity screening in a low-prevalence stream produces a **high false-positive rate by design** (NICE flags this for the Whooley case-finder), and each false positive carries assessment burden and can distress a not-at-risk person. So a signed look-alike boundary isn't optional polish — it's the specificity control. [NICE common mental health disorders (PMC3252532)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3252532); [Whooley meta-analysis (PMC4679987)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679987/).

## CD3 — "I feel numb" (and the oracle edges): do NOT auto-route; insert one clarifying turn; suppress BA in acute grief
**Recommendation (strongly evidence-supported): exclude "I feel numb" / "I feel stuck" / "I don't feel like myself" from BA-enrichment, and on isolated numbness ask one low-pressure clarifying question before choosing a pathway; if acute grief is indicated, suppress behavioral-activation prompts and default to validation/stabilization.**
Evidence: emotional numbness is a **shared, non-discriminating** feature of acute grief *and* depression — it's a hallmark of early normal grief, a core symptom of **Prolonged Grief Disorder** (DSM-5-TR, 2022), and appears in depression as affective blunting; you cannot route it reliably from the phrase alone. BA for *acute, fresh* grief is **suboptimal-to-invalidating** (evidence-based grief protocols place BA later and only for *complicated/prolonged* grief; acute-grief goals are stabilization, not activation scheduling). The grief/depression boundary itself is contested (DSM-5 bereavement-exclusion removal). This is exactly why blindly enriching BA's anchors to "catch numb" would route grieving people into behavioral activation — a clinical error, not a routing side-effect. [PGD in DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 (PMC10291380)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10291380/); [CBT for complicated grief (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1077722923000949); [Grief vs MDD controversy (AAFP)](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2014/1115/p690.html).

## CD4 — Set a TWO-TIER recall standard: fail-safe screen ~80–90%, acute-SI catch near-zero-miss
**Recommendation: accept the §3a-eligibility screen in the ~80–90% sensitivity band *provided every miss falls through to genuine supportive care*; hold a separate, far-higher-recall bar for the acute SI-answer catch, because a miss there is not fail-safe.**
Evidence: there is **no universal numeric threshold**; validated first-line screens are deliberately tuned high-sensitivity/low-specificity in the **~80–97%** range (PHQ-2 ≥2 sens ~0.91; PHQ-2→PHQ-9 ~0.82; PHQ-9 ≥10 ~0.88; ASQ ~0.97). NICE frames these as **case-finding gates** whose positives must fall through to fuller assessment. Decisively: screening theory tolerates sub-100% sensitivity *because a screen is one node in a chain* — a **missed screen that falls through to support is fail-safe**, which is precisely your architecture, so a 65–90% first-pass is clinically defensible there. That tolerance does **not** transfer to acute/active suicide risk, where a miss is not fail-safe and the recall bar collapses toward zero — which is exactly why the crisis-firing decision is the deterministic SI-*answer* catch, not the probabilistic eligibility signal. (Note: USPSTF-2023 gives depression screening a B recommendation but only an "I statement" for adult suicide-risk screening — the SI question should be justified locally, which this flow does via the §3a symptom trigger.) [PHQ IPD meta-analysis (PMC7284301)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7284301/); [NICE (PMC3252532)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3252532); [USPSTF 2023 (JAMA)](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2806144).

## CD5 — Approve "err toward NOT asking on ambiguous/benign cases" — but on the CORRECT rationale
**Recommendation: gate the SI question behind meaningful §3a signal (don't ask clearly-benign/informational users, don't repeat within a session), and record the rationale as specificity + false-positive burden + trust — explicitly NOT "asking about suicide is harmful."**
Evidence: the strongest, most replicated finding is that **asking about SI does not increase ideation** (Dazzi 2014; Blades 2018; DeCou & Schumann; a staggered repeated-screening study — some signals of slight *decrease*). So the "asking is dangerous" framing is false and would not survive clinical review. The *real* costs of over-asking are **false-positive cascade** (moderate evidence), and **burden/alarm-fatigue/trust erosion** under repeated asking (weaker — inferred from research-participant burden studies, not chatbot studies; label as inference). [DeCou & Schumann (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/sltb.12368); [Blades 2018 (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735818301351); [repeated-screening study (PubMed 29486340)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29486340/).
